# Three rivals leave the self-hosted niche open

The founder can compete, but only for a narrow set of buyers, and not by being a better Tuleap, Flow or Stell. None of the three is a direct substitute: Tuleap sells a configurable ALM platform whose document-style requirements view is paid-only, Flow sells an engineering-data and AI platform to funded hardware companies, and Stell sells an accredited US government cloud for turning contract PDFs into compliance matrices. The opening they share is a document-first requirements tool that a small team installs on its own server, offline, with baselines, deterministic suspect links, a trace matrix and ReqIF or Word exchange in the free edition. That opening is real for non-US defence and aerospace suppliers, for firms that already run an isolated or on-premises enclave, and for document-centric teams who find Tuleap too heavy. It is not real for venture-backed hardware startups, who choose Flow because it is not Jama-like, or for US subcontractors with no IT staff, who are now better served by Stell's certified cloud. Three strategic facts have changed since the earlier research: Enalean states it has joined Dassault Systèmes, Flow raised a $50M Series B at a $750M valuation on 30 September 2026, and Stell added CMMC Level 2 and FedRAMP Moderate Equivalency in 2026. Most of the "missing feature" findings for Flow and Stell mean "not in public material", because neither publishes a user manual, so the founder must confirm them by trial and demo before saying them in public. The larger competitor for these buyers is still Word and Excel, and no evidence was found on what 10 to 50 person subcontractors actually buy.

## Tuleap's Jama-like layer is paid, beta and tied to Agile milestones

**What it sells, and to whom.** Tuleap is a generic tracker engine sold as a full ALM platform: requirements are artifacts in a configurable tracker, alongside agile planning, Git, test management and document management. Enalean is a company of about 20 people founded in 2011 in the French Alps, with an aggregator-estimated $2.4M revenue ([Latka](https://getlatka.com/companies/tuleap.org)). Its customer stories are a 300-person ISO 13485 software firm, an automotive lighting supplier, a robotics company and the French statistics office, and its compliance claims centre on ISO 26262, ASPICE and DO-178C ([Tuleap requirements page](https://www.tuleap.com/product/features/requirement-baseline)). The About page now states that **Enalean has joined Dassault Systèmes and the CATIA organisation**; no date, terms or statement on the Community Edition's future was found ([About Tuleap](https://www.tuleap.com/about-tuleap/)). Tuleap is therefore becoming the ALM piece of a PLM suite sold to primes and large industrial suppliers.

**Where not to compete.** Do not compete on breadth, configurability, price per seat or reputation. Tuleap covers far more than requirements, and users praise the ability to shape their own forms and workflows. Its cloud price is low: **EUR 13 per user per month** on myTuleap, capped at 30 users, which a new tool cannot meaningfully undercut ([Tuleap pricing](https://www.tuleap.com/pricing/), seen through a search summary and matched by [Softwarefinder](https://softwarefinder.com/project-management-software/tuleap)). Reviews are few and positive, 4.5 out of 5 on Capterra from 37 reviews, so a pitch built on "Tuleap is disliked" has no support ([Capterra](https://www.capterra.com/p/175234/Tuleap/reviews/)). The Dassault channel will also win large aerospace accounts that a solo founder could never reach.

Two cautions apply. First, Tuleap's source is GPL, so "Enterprise-only" most likely means "not packaged or supported in the free distribution" and not "closed"; a determined team could build the plugins. That is an inference and was not tested. Second, the gap is closing: Enalean's release notes call Artidoc "the bedrock of Tuleap Requirement Management" ([Tuleap 15.10](https://help.tuleap.com/docs/tuleap-15-10.md)). Tuleap is moving toward the founder's design, inside the paid edition. The earlier research also recorded Tuleap marketing itself as usable in air-gapped environments ([Tuleap landing page](https://content.tuleap.org/lp-ads-en-request-demo)), which conflicts with the installer's stated need for Internet access; the install docs are the stronger source, and the conflict is unresolved.

**The angle.** Win on fit and footprint: "requirements management that works on day one, free, on one server". A team using free Tuleap gets generic trackers and links and must configure everything; the Jama-like document experience, a document baseline and an easy production install all sit behind a sales call. The founder's tool gives a document-first view, per-set UIDs, named baselines of a set and a single app image plus Postgres, with no feature removed from the free edition. For a team of three to ten that needs SSO or on-premises support, Tuleap's 25-user pack is also a poor fit. Being independent of a PLM vendor is a fair secondary point, but the idea that Dassault will neglect the Community Edition is speculation and should not be stated as fact.

## Flow sells live engineering data to funded teams, not specifications

**What it sells, and to whom.** Flow is a "systems graph" that links requirements to live design values, CAD, code and test data, with Git-style branches and AI agents on top ([Flow](https://www.flowengineering.com/)). Its buyers are engineering leaders at well-funded hardware companies and now large automotive: Rivian grew from 40 to 1,500 users in seven months, and new customers include General Motors, Anduril and Stoke Space. The CEO says 96% of customers arrive inbound. **The Series B was $50M at a $750M valuation, announced 30 September 2026**, bringing the total raised to about $81.5M by the researchers' sum ([Flow press release](https://www.flowengineering.com/blog/series-b-press-release)). A valuation of that size commits Flow to a broad platform story and makes a move down-market to cheap document-style RM unlikely.

**Where not to compete.** Do not compete on live design values and budgets, CAD and test integrations, AI agents, branch-and-merge change control, API breadth or delivery speed. Flow publishes about 200 API endpoints and an MCP server ([API index](https://docs.flowengineering.com/llms.txt)) and ships weekly ([release notes](https://docs.flowengineering.com/release-notes)). Its buyers choose it precisely because it is not Jama-like, so a Jama-style interface is a disadvantage with them. Two comfortable assumptions are also false. **Flow is not cloud-only**: its security page offers "cloud, GovCloud, or fully self-hosted" and claims SOC 2 Type II plus ITAR, EAR and NIST 800-171 alignment ([Flow security](https://www.flowengineering.com/security)). And Flow is not weak on formal change control: version history, revert, diffs, required reviewers, review policy, an audit log and ReqIF import all appear in its API index or release notes.

| Basic is $150 per editor per month, capped at 500 requirements and 1 project; Pro is $300, capped at 5,000 and 5 | **Confirmed by vendor** | [Flow pricing](https://www.flowengineering.com/pricing) |

On list prices, five editors cost $9,000 a year on Basic and $18,000 on Pro, and a real programme passes 500 requirements quickly. The tier a small defence firm would need for ITAR hosting has no public price. Flow most likely handles "what changed and what does it affect" through impact analysis on a branch before merge, which is a different approach to suspect links, not proof that the capability is missing. Its "self-hosted" option is probably a vendor-managed deployment into the customer's own cloud account, since nothing about installing or upgrading it is published; that too is an inference.

**The angle.** Win where Flow is structurally unsuited or uninterested: true self-hosting with no outbound calls and no AI dependency; non-US suppliers, who gain nothing from US GovCloud with US-persons-only access; small budgets; and **pinned releases the customer upgrades on its own schedule**. That last point matters to teams that must validate or qualify their tools, for whom a product that changes every week is a cost. Add a classic, auditor-shaped record: named immutable baselines, a trace matrix, suspect flags, sign-off records and Word or PDF specification export. The honest read is that Flow overlaps with the founder mainly on the word "requirements". Flow's neglect of small subcontractors is neglect, not inability: the Basic tier is open to anyone, and FedRAMP money will remove the government-cloud objection for larger US buyers within a year or two.

## Stell's moat is its US accreditation stack, not its feature set

**What it sells, and to whom.** Stell sells "AI-native requirements management" for US aerospace and defence hardware teams, and names Jama, DOORS, Polarion, Word and Excel as what teams switch from ([Stell](https://www.stell-engineering.com/)). Its centre of gravity is the inbound customer document: import a PDF contract, specification or standard, turn it into a compliance matrix, attach verification evidence and share it read-only with customers and suppliers ([Stell product](https://www.stell-engineering.com/product)). The prime-to-supplier flow-down network of its 2023 pitch is not visible in the 2026 product; what remains is a read-only sharing portal. Confirmed users are US government space organisations reached through SBIR contracts worth about $7M, roughly equal to all the venture capital it has raised, and no commercial customer is named ([Stell, Space RCO contract](https://stell-engineering.com/blog/stell-awarded-space-rco-contract-to-deploy-software-for-rapid-systems-integration)). No round after the March 2024 seed was found. Stell markets to small subcontractors, but the public evidence of traction is government programme offices and new-space companies.

**Where not to compete.** Do not compete as a hosted service for controlled unclassified information. Stell runs entirely on AWS GovCloud with an all-US-person team, and claims **an active IL5 ATO under the US Space Force, SOC 2 Type II, CMMC Level 2 certification (31 March 2026) and FedRAMP Moderate Equivalency assessed by a 3PAO (October 2026)** ([Stell security](https://stell-engineering.com/security), [press release](https://www.24-7pressrelease.com/press-release/539368/stell-engineering-achieves-fedramp-moderate-equivalency-for-its-requirements-management-platform)). These are vendor claims read from press releases and were not independently verified, and equivalency is not a FedRAMP authorisation. Even so, a solo founder cannot fund that stack. For a US shop with no IT staff that is allowed to hold CUI in a compliant cloud, Stell beats self-hosting. Do not attack its AI import of PDF, Word and Excel into a compliance matrix, its per-requirement evidence tracking or its standards library either; the founder's tool has no equivalent of the first.

**What it lacks or gates.** Stell has no public documentation, changelog, trial or price, so almost every gap here is "undocumented".

| Gap | Evidence status | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Hosted on AWS GovCloud (US) only, with a US-person-only team | **Confirmed by vendor** | [Stell security](https://stell-engineering.com/security) |
| No public price, tier, trial or self-serve sign-up; fees can change on 30 days' notice | **Confirmed** from the site and terms | [Stell terms](https://www.stell-engineering.com/legal/terms-of-service) |
| Customer data retrievable for 30 days after termination; export format unstated | **Confirmed** from terms | [Stell terms](https://www.stell-engineering.com/legal/terms-of-service) |
| AI output is "as-is"; the customer must verify parsed requirements | **Confirmed** from terms | [Stell terms](https://www.stell-engineering.com/legal/terms-of-service) |
| Self-hosted, on-premises or air-gapped install | **Not mentioned** on any page; a third-party analysis also found none | [Airframe.ai](https://www.airframe.ai/product/stell-engineering-com/analysis) |
| ReqIF, a DOORS importer, any export format | **Not mentioned**, including in Stell's own DOORS-migration article | [Stell, DOORS alternatives](https://stell-engineering.com/blog/ibm-doors-alternatives) |
| Named baselines, per-item revision diff, rule-based suspect links, user-defined link types, signed reviews | **Not mentioned**; version comparison and change impact are presented as AI queries | [Stell Zelda](https://www.stell-engineering.com/zelda) |
| Whether the API can write | **Unknown**; described as read-oriented | [Stell product](https://www.stell-engineering.com/product) |

Stell itself credits DOORS with "comprehensive versioning and baseline management" and Jama with structured reviews, while claiming only "complete audit history" for its own product ([Stell, best RM tools](https://stell-engineering.com/blog/best-requirements-management-tools)). That is suggestive, not proof. Stell could add ReqIF, export and baselines within a quarter if customers ask, so those are not durable advantages. Self-hosting, open source and service to non-US customers are much harder for it to copy, because its AI runs on AWS Bedrock inside the GovCloud boundary and its authorisations attach to that boundary.

**The angle.** Self-hosting changes the compliance question instead of answering it. Under the CMMC rule a contractor may use a cloud provider that is FedRAMP Moderate authorised or equivalent, and the contractor's own infrastructure is covered by its own assessment ([32 CFR 170.16](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/32/170.16)). Software inside the customer's boundary therefore needs no vendor FedRAMP status. The founder can win the customer whose prime, contract or security officer forbids third-party cloud, the customer on an isolated network, and every Canadian, UK, EU or Australian supplier that Stell does not serve at all. AGPL self-hosting also answers Stell's exit and continuity weaknesses: closed source, a 30-day retrieval window and a seed-stage vendor on programmes that run for decades. The counterweights are serious. The subcontractor with no IT staff is the one least able to self-host. US defence buyers will ask a solo open-source vendor about its supply chain, an SBOM, FIPS-validated cryptography and who can commit code, and Stell's "100% US-person team" line is aimed at exactly that fear. A solo founder has the same continuity problem as a seed-stage company; only the licence mitigates it.

## The founder can win non-US and enclave buyers, not startups

**Verdict.** The founder can compete for four kinds of buyer and should concede the rest. The best fit is **small defence and aerospace suppliers outside the US**, who are excluded by Stell and unhelped by Flow's US GovCloud. The second is **firms of any country that already run an on-premises or isolated enclave**, or whose security officer refuses third-party cloud. The third is **document-centric teams priced or sized out of the alternatives**: too small for Tuleap's 25-user pack and its platform footprint, and unwilling to pay $300 per editor per month for Flow Pro. The fourth, **small medical device teams**, is open only relative to these three rivals: Flow shows no medical customers, Tuleap shows no Part 11 or validation-pack story, and Stell does not address the sector. The earlier research still applies there. Medtech's real rivals are Matrix Requirements, Ketryx, Greenlight Guru and Jama, which bundle validation documents and e-signatures, and small medtech buys cloud tools sold as "no IT required" ([Matrix One](https://matrixone.health/pricing), [MedDeviceGuide](https://meddeviceguide.com/blog/best-eqms-software-medical-devices-2026-guide)). Defence remains the better first segment for a self-hosted product.

The founder will lose venture-backed hardware startups to Flow, US subcontractors without IT staff to Stell, and prime-level accounts to Dassault and the incumbents. None of the three rivals is the main obstacle for the buyers that remain. The realistic default for a 10 to 50 person subcontractor is Word and Excel, a point supported only by a press remark that engineers "often use Excel, Word, or Jira as workarounds" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/03/11/stell-raises-new-tranche-of-funding-to-keep-engineers-building-not-filling-out-paperwork/)). No survey or forum data was found on what these firms buy or pay, how many must work air-gapped, or how often they receive requirements as ReqIF and not PDF. The size of the opening is therefore unknown.

**Features these gaps argue for.** The priorities below are the researchers' judgement from the gaps above, ranked by how hard each is for the rivals to copy.

| Priority | Why these rivals make it matter |
|---|---|
| Offline-installable single image plus Postgres, no outbound calls, pinned releases with clear upgrade notes | Tuleap's install needs Internet, SELinux off and five services; Flow's self-hosting is undocumented; Stell has none. This is the one advantage all three would struggle to copy |
| Security paperwork for the customer's own assessment: SBOM, hardening guide, audit log mapped to NIST 800-171 controls, FIPS crypto option | A buyer inside a CMMC boundary will ask for these; without them the self-hosting argument fails at the security officer |
| Named immutable baselines of a set, with baseline-to-baseline comparison | Tuleap's is beta and milestone-bound; not found at Flow or Stell in classic form |
| Deterministic suspect links, pulled forward from Phase 2 | Not found at any of the three; Flow and Stell answer with AI or branch impact analysis, which an auditor cannot read as a rule |
| ReqIF import and export, plus Word, PDF and Excel export of specifications and the trace matrix | Flow shows import only; Stell and Tuleap show neither. Cheap for rivals to add, so ship it early while it is a difference |
| Import from Word and Excel, a verification-method and evidence field per requirement, and a compliance-matrix view | Stell's best feature is intake of a prime's documents. An RM tool with only manual or CSV entry will look dated to the same buyers |
| Signed reviews and a validation pack per pinned release | No e-signature found at Tuleap, Flow or Stell; required for medtech and useful for defence sign-off |
| Every RM feature in the free edition, a public price list, no seat minimum | Tuleap gates Artidoc and sells 25-user packs; Flow gates SSO and GovCloud; Stell publishes nothing |

AI agents, CAD and simulation connectors, live design values, branching and a hosted CUI cloud should stay off the roadmap. The one AI question worth an explicit decision is document import, because Stell, Flow and Trace.Space all lead with it and it is becoming an expected feature; a self-hosted tool cannot call an external model on an air-gapped site, so a good non-AI Word and Excel import is the safer first step.

## Trials and demos must settle what public pages cannot

Every "not found" above needs a first-hand check before the founder uses it in a pitch. The Flow trial is the cheapest and most valuable, because Flow's public documentation covers only its API.

| Check | How | What it settles |
|---|---|---|
| Install Tuleap Community Edition from the Docker image | Half a day, hands-on | Whether baseline, cross-tracker search, LDAP, OIDC, docx export and test management are present; how long a requirements tracker takes to configure; whether an offline install is possible at all |
| Read Tuleap's artifact-links docs and try link types | Hands-on; the page returned 404 during research | Whether custom link types and any change flagging exist |
| Search the Dassault Systèmes newsroom and Tuleap's blog | Desk check | Acquisition date and terms, and any statement on the Community Edition and GPL licence |
| Ask Enalean sales for an on-premises quote for five users | Email | Real on-premises price and minimum pack; whether a validation or tool-qualification kit exists; whether Artidoc documents can be baselined |
| Sign up for Flow's free trial | Hands-on, two weeks | Whether a named frozen baseline, a trace matrix view, suspect or stale flags, Word export and ReqIF export exist; whether review approval records who signed and when |
| Ask Flow sales about self-hosting for a ten-person firm | Sales call | Price, who operates it, whether it runs air-gapped, whether AI features work offline, and whether non-US hosting is offered |
| Book Stell's 30-minute demo | Sales call | Baselines, per-item history, export formats, ReqIF, API write access, private deployment on request, pricing basis, and whether suppliers can respond in the portal |
| Ask Stell for the body of evidence behind its FedRAMP equivalency | Sales call | Whether the claim holds up; advisers warn that such claims are sometimes made without a full assessment ([Kiteworks](https://www.kiteworks.com/regulatory-compliance/beware-fedramp-equivalency-claims/)) |
| Read the DoD equivalency memo and 32 CFR 170 in the original | Desk check | The self-hosting compliance argument, which rests on secondary summaries |

The interviews matter more than the trials, because the buyer evidence is the weakest part of this research. Ask small defence subcontractors in what format their prime sends requirements and in what format they must return compliance: ReqIF, a DOORS export, or PDF, Word and Excel. Ask whether their contract or security officer allows a FedRAMP-equivalent cloud for CUI, or requires on-premises or isolated working. Ask who would install and maintain a self-hosted tool, and whether they have that person today. Ask what they use now and what it costs, whether they have evaluated Flow, Stell or Tuleap and why they did or did not buy, and what security documents they would demand from a one-person open-source vendor. Ask whether an auditor or customer has ever requested a suspect-link report, a signed baseline or a trace matrix by name. Put the same questions on tools, cost and sign-off to small medical device teams, and ask them whether they would accept self-hosting at all.

## Conclusion

The founder's worry assumes three rivals for the same customer. The research shows three companies pulled away from that customer by their own money: Tuleap toward Dassault's industrial accounts, Flow toward the large seat counts a $750M valuation requires, and Stell toward the US government programmes that fund it. The small, document-centric team that must run its own software is the one buyer none of them is paid to serve. That makes the niche available. It does not make it large, and the earlier finding stands that nobody has yet shown willingness to pay.

The durable advantages are structural, not functional. Baselines, ReqIF and export are features a funded rival adds in a quarter. Offline self-hosting, open source, pinned versions and availability outside the US are things Stell's architecture, Flow's release model and Tuleap's platform weight make hard to copy. The founder should build the features because buyers need them, and sell the structure because rivals cannot follow. The next step is the checks in the previous section, since the report's most useful claims about Flow and Stell rest on what their websites do not say.
