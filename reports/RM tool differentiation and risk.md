# Self-hosting sells to defence, not medtech

Of the six differentiation assumptions, only one holds cleanly (rivals hide prices), three partly hold, and two are wrong as stated: Matrix Requirements and Ketryx both sell on-premises on their Enterprise tier, and Tuleap is already an open-source, self-hostable, GUI-based tool marketed at medical devices. The gap that survives is narrower: no mature, adopted, RM-only, Jama-style open-source web tool exists, and no web-based, self-hostable RM tool is priced for teams under ten seats. The larger problem is fit. Small medtech buys cloud tools sold as "no IT required" and complains about price, lock-in and export, while explicit self-hosting demand sits in aerospace and defence. Scope expansion does not fix profitability: e-signed reviews, risk items and minimal test results are entry requirements that competitors bundle at no extra charge, and eQMS or full ALM puts a solo founder against vendors with $50M to $120M raised. The business is viable only as a small one, realistically 10 to 30 enterprise licences, and its potentially fatal risks are unproven willingness to pay, a cloud tier that cannot fund its own compliance costs, and a free/paid boundary that either cripples the free edition or removes the reason to pay. Most of this evidence is thin: search-result summaries, vendor and competitor blogs, and procurement samples of four to seven deals, with no first-hand buyer voice at all. The founder's planned interviews now matter more than any further desk research.

## Only one of six differentiation assumptions survives intact

| Assumption | Verdict | Corrected claim the evidence supports |
|---|---|---|
| Medtech rivals are cloud-only | **Wrong** (true only for Greenlight Guru) | Cloud-first; self-hosting gated to the most expensive tier |
| Nothing else is open source, self-hostable and GUI-based | **Wrong as worded; a narrower gap is real** | The only RM-only, Jama-style open-source tool, if it reaches maturity |
| Rivals hide prices | **Holds** | Enterprise and medtech rivals need a sales call |
| Rivals charge for reviewer seats | **Partly holds; false for Jama** | Free reviewers without a ten-creator base package |
| Small teams mostly use Word/Excel | **Partly holds; unquantified** | Word/Excel or Jira plus documents |
| Small teams value self-hosting and open source | **Wrong on the evidence found** | They value low price, no lock-in and data export |
| Buyers pay for a validation pack, e-signatures, SSO | **Partly holds; weak** | Rivals bundle the first two; SSO gating is normal but resented |
| Test tools are not competitors; RM-only is acceptable | **Partly holds** | Not competitors, but RM without linked risk and test records looks incomplete |

**Cloud-only is wrong for two of the three named rivals.** Ketryx's own pricing page says "Enterprise plans can also be deployed single-tenant or on-premises" ([Ketryx](https://www.ketryx.com/pricing)). Matrix Requirements' documentation lists on-premises and a VPN-only private cloud instance under its Enterprise subscription ([Matrix docs](https://docs23.matrixreq.com/usv23/what-are-the-hosting-options-can-i-install-on-prem)). The notes disagree here and the conflict is unresolved: that manual is dated 2024, the current Matrix One pricing page does not mention on-premises ([Matrix One](https://matrixone.health/pricing)), and a third-party review calls Matrix Quality cloud-only ([SoftwareConnect](https://softwareconnect.com/reviews/matrix-quality/)). Greenlight Guru looks cloud-only, but on a single third-party aggregator ([rfp.wiki](https://www.rfp.wiki/specialty-industries/manufacturing/quality-management-system-software/greenlight-guru/qualio)). Among enterprise tools self-hosting is the norm: Polarion sells both on-premise and cloud ([Siemens](https://www.siemens.com/en-us/products/polarion/)), and Jama still ships a self-hosted release with an air-gapped install path, though it steers customers to cloud ([Jama support](https://support.jamasoftware.com/hc/en-us/articles/41248672999181-Installing-and-Upgrading-Jama-Connect-v9-28-x-KOTS-only)). "Self-hosted" alone therefore differentiates against nobody important. What does differentiate is self-hosting a three-person team can run, on every tier, with no seat minimum.

**The open-source claim fails on Tuleap.** Tuleap is GPL-licensed, web-based, covers requirements and risks, and is sold in the cloud and on-premises with medical device marketing ([Wikipedia](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuleap), [Tuleap](https://www.tuleap.com/pricing/)). Two small projects started in late 2024 make the founder's exact pitch: ReqTrackManager, a one-person GPL-3.0 "DOORS alternative" with zero stars, and OpenRose with 18 ([ReqTrackManager](https://github.com/ballardr/reqtrackmanager), [OpenRose](https://github.com/OpenRose/OpenRose)). What does not exist is a mature RM-only web GUI tool: every healthy dedicated open-source RM project is text-file based, and the tool that listicles still recommend for this need, OSRMT, has had no commits since 2020 ([OSRMT](https://github.com/osrmt/osrmt)). One caveat cuts in the founder's favour and is unchecked: nobody verified which Tuleap features sit in the free Community Edition.

**Hidden prices hold; paid reviewers does not.** Only ReqView, Tuleap's cloud tiers and Atlassian Marketplace apps publish real prices; Ketryx publishes only its free tier, and Jama, Polarion, Codebeamer, Greenlight Guru and Qualio all require a sales conversation ([ReqView](https://www.reqview.com/pricing/), [Ketryx](https://www.ketryx.com/pricing), [G2 on Jama](https://www.g2.com/products/jama-connect/pricing)). But Jama, the reference product, bundles site licences for stakeholders and reviewers with its base package of up to ten named creators ([Jama licensing](https://www.jamasoftware.com/datasheet/jama-connect-licensing)), and Matrix One says read-only users are free on most plans ([Matrix One](https://matrixone.health/pricing)). Free reviewers is parity, not a headline. The honest contrast is against Jama's floor: the lowest Jama deal in Vendr's seven-purchase sample was **$56,311 a year** ([Vendr](https://vendr.com/marketplace/jama-software)).

**Word and Excel is plausible, and self-hosting demand is not.** The only numbers on spreadsheet use come from surveys commissioned by vendors selling RM tools, with no breakdown by company size ([Axendia](https://axendia.com/blog/2022/01/31/new-market-research-the-costly-impact-of-ineffective-requirements-management-in-the-medical-device-industry/)). For software-led teams, Jira is at least as likely an incumbent; Ketryx's whole product keeps requirements "in Jira or the tool of your choice" ([Ketryx](https://www.ketryx.com/capabilities/requirements-management)). On self-hosting, no preference data exists in either direction, so the verdict rests on supply: every tool built for small medtech is cloud-first, and buyer guides praise "rapid cloud deployment with no IT infrastructure required" ([MedDeviceGuide](https://meddeviceguide.com/blog/best-eqms-software-medical-devices-2026-guide)). The documented complaints are opaque pricing and year-long lock-in, reported by a competitor ([Atlassian community, written by OpenRegulatory](https://community.atlassian.com/forums/App-Central-discussions/How-To-Set-Up-a-Jira-Confluence-QMS-For-Medical-Device/m-p/2953280)). Open source helps as an exit guarantee, not as a pull factor: several tiny open-source medtech QMS projects show little visible adoption.

**The paid features are mostly entry requirements.** Matrix One includes IQ/OQ/PQ validation documentation, Part 11 e-signatures and audit trails in the subscription with "no hidden fees or per-feature add-ons" ([Matrix One](https://matrixone.health/pricing)), and Ketryx ships a validation package with every release ([Ketryx](https://www.ketryx.com/blog/inside-ketryxs-customer-validation-package)). SSO gating is commercially normal: of 1,112 SaaS tools offering SSO, 61% restrict it to the top tier ([Toolradar](https://toolradar.com/reports/saas-feature-paywall-2026)), though GitLab gives basic SAML SSO to free self-managed users ([GitLab](https://docs.gitlab.com/integration/saml)). The validation pack remains the most defensible paid item because it is recurring work rather than code and cannot be forked. No source found a price for a standalone pack.

**RM-only is half right.** Nothing in the notes shows Qase or TestRail competing for this buyer, and Jama's own recommended pattern keeps Jira separate and syncs to it ([Jama](https://www.jamasoftware.com/blog/jama-connect-features-in-five-jama-connect-interchange-what-sets-us-apart/)). But every medtech competitor sells the trace from requirement to risk control to verification as the product, and a competitor's review dismisses the requirements-only tool Doorstop as "not really QMS software" ([OpenRegulatory](https://openregulatory.com/articles/qms-software-iso-13485-comparison)). No buyer statement was found rejecting a tool for lacking risk or test, so this rests on what rivals bundle. The real substitutes at the bottom are near-free: Xray on Jira Cloud at about $100 a year for ten users ([Atlassian Community](https://community.atlassian.com/forums/App-Central-questions/How-does-X-Ray-Flat-Fee-work-Same-with-Zephyr/qaq-p/3191570)), Ketryx's $0 pre-market plan, and ReqView at EUR 440 per user per year.

## Wider scope raises the win rate, not the margin

Large outcomes belong to vendors that sell more than RM, but the causation is unproven. Jama sold for **$1.2B** with test management and review alongside requirements ([Francisco Partners](https://www.franciscopartners.com/media/francisco-partners-to-acquire-jama-software-for-12b)); Greenlight Guru took $120M as a QMS-first vendor ([MassDevice](https://www.massdevice.com/jmi-equity-invests-120m-in-greenlight-guru/)). The RM-centred exit with a disclosed price, Valispace, went for **$15.6M upfront** and became a feature of Altium's suite, now priced at $995 a year with unlimited collaborators ([Altium ASX filing](https://announcements.asx.com.au/asxpdf/20240227/pdf/060vg3c47dl0zj.pdf), [Valispace](https://www.valispace.com/pricing/)). Those companies also had age, sales teams and funding, so the data cannot separate "scope caused growth" from "growth paid for scope". Analyst market sizes are no help: firms disagree by nearly 3x on the RM market in the same year.

The counterexample is decisive for an open-source founder. **Tuleap has full ALM scope, an open-source licence, about fifteen years of history, and an estimated $2.4M revenue with 22 staff**, a headcount roughly flat since 2016 ([Latka](https://getlatka.com/companies/tuleap.org), [Le Journal des Entreprises](https://www.lejournaldesentreprises.com/article/alliance-entre-sogilis-et-enalean-56998)). The revenue figure is an aggregator estimate, not a filing. Matrix Requirements, the nearest medtech precedent, took eight years to reach 180 customers with ALM and QMS products, then sold a majority stake for **EUR 10M** ([Lauxera](https://lauxera.com/lauxera-capital-partners-invests-e10m-in-german-medtech-software-company-matrix-requirements/)). That is a good founder outcome and a realistic ceiling for the niche.

The price uplift from scope is real but is not a module surcharge. A five-person team pays about EUR 3,000 a year on ReqView and a reported $25k to $35k on Greenlight Guru ([ReqView](https://www.reqview.com/pricing/), [OpenRegulatory](https://openregulatory.com/articles/greenlight-guru-price)). The difference buys a hosted, validated platform with mandatory onboarding, sold to a compliance buyer. An AGPL self-hosted edition gives away precisely that platform fee, and no per-module price for risk, test or e-signatures was found at any vendor.

The expansions rank as follows; the ordering is researcher judgement, since no source gives build cost per module. **Design reviews with e-signatures are worth it and should move earlier than Phase 3**: a review is a baseline plus approvals, and without it a regulated team cannot use the tool as its record. **Risk management is worth it in minimal form**: a risk is an item type with computed scores linked to requirements as controls. **Test management is worth it only as recorded results**, not a runner: a test case item plus an immutable imported result closes the trace matrix, while a TestRail-style executor is a product in itself. **eQMS and document control are not worth it**: Matrix sells QMS as a separate product to a different buyer, and the field holds Greenlight Guru and Qualio with $50M or more each ([Fenwick](https://www.fenwick.com/insights/experience/fenwick-represents-qualio-in-50-million-series-b-financing)). **Full ALM is not worth it**: it contradicts the product's one clear point of difference from Tuleap. The first three raise win rate and base price. None adds a second revenue line, so expansion improves the odds of a sale far more than it improves profitability.

## Three risks can kill the business; the rest are overhead

**Potentially fatal: nobody has shown willingness to pay.** Demand evidence is supply-side throughout. Direct requests for an open-source Jama alternative amount to a few Hacker News posts with single-digit points over ten years ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=12794843)), and Reddit, where buyers talk, could not be searched. The historical record is poor: RM-only open-source GUI tools (OSRMT, Eclipse ProR, the Redmine RE plugin) stalled when the maintainer or grant stopped, and survivors have an employer paying for maintenance. The only cure is evidence from customers: finish the planned interviews and get paid design-partner commitments before building further compliance features.

**Potentially fatal: the cloud tier cannot carry its costs.** At $30 to $40 per editor per month and about five editors, a customer is worth roughly $1,800 to $2,400 a year, so $100k ARR needs 42 to 56 customers (researcher arithmetic). A first SOC 2 Type II cycle is quoted at roughly $20k to $60k all-in by vendor blogs with an interest in the answer ([Beancount](https://beancount.io/blog/2026/07/14/soc-2-type-ii-small-saas-audit-cost-guide), [Startup Defense](https://www.startupdefense.io/soc-2-costs-for-startups-complete-breakdown-and-budget-guide)). Each regulated customer also brings a supplier questionnaire and a quality agreement, because ISO 13485 clause 7.4 requires risk-based supplier evaluation ([Johner Institute](https://blog.johner-institute.com/quality-management-iso-13485/supplier-evaluation-supplier-selection-supplier-audits/)); that cost does not shrink with deal size. The price is also far below Matrix's cheapest reported plan of about EUR 610 a month for three seats ([OpenRegulatory](https://openregulatory.com/articles/matrix-requirements-pricing)), low enough to signal immaturity. The business is in practice 10 to 30 enterprise licences. The fix is to raise the hosted price to a flat team plan nearer the market floor, defer certification until customers demand it, and say so openly.

**Potentially fatal: the free/paid boundary.** Every target buyer needs audit trail and controlled approval. Gate them and the free edition is a demo; give them away and a frugal startup never pays. The most defensible line keeps history and audit trail free and charges for Part 11 signatures, formal review workflow, SSO and the validation pack, accepting that some teams will sign off outside the tool forever. Decide it once and never move a feature from free to paid, since the relicensing backlashes all followed that kind of change ([The New Stack](https://thenewstack.io/what-happens-to-relicensed-open-source-projects-and-their-forks/)).

**Serious but manageable: trust in a one-person vendor.** Rivals use their own ISO 13485 certification as a selling point, and a QA lead must justify an unknown supplier to an auditor. Auditors assess the manufacturer's validation records, not the brand: in a real nonconformity quoted on Elsmar Cove, practitioners concluded the gap was "not with the specific tool" ([Elsmar](https://elsmar.com/elsmarqualityforum/threads/validation-of-software-regarding-iso-13485-2016.89461/page-2)). Self-hosting helps less than hoped here, because installation qualification and revalidation after every upgrade fall on the customer. Open source plus self-hosting does answer the continuity question better than escrow. The mitigation is to behave like a supplier: release-pinned validation evidence, a documented development process, and one or two customers who passed a notified body audit using the tool.

**Manageable: competition and licence friction.** Ketryx raised a **$39M Series B** in September 2025 ([Ketryx](https://www.ketryx.com/blog/ketryx-raises-39m-series-b-from-transformation-lightspeed-to-scale-ai-in-medtech)) and Jama has a startup package, but incumbents are raising prices, and Matrix removed its entry plan, which opens the low end. That low end is already contested by OpenRegulatory's Formwork, which has a free tier and a consulting-led channel a solo developer lacks. AI-built look-alikes will keep appearing, so the moat is trust and distribution, not code. AGPL lands on corporate stop lists, which usually means an approval step and not a ban ([Heather Meeker](https://heathermeeker.com/2023/10/13/agpl-in-the-light-of-day/)); the commercial licence answers it. Expect almost no outside contributions regardless of the CLA. Early-stage medtech funding is also deteriorating, with 647 deals in the first nine months of 2025 against 948 in all of 2024 ([MedTech Dive](https://www.medtechdive.com/news/medtech-vc-funding-slows-in-q3-pitchbook/805096/)), which shrinks the pool of new companies setting up a toolchain.

No comparable was found for a solo-founder open-core company selling to regulated industry. Plausible Analytics needed two founders, a horizontal market and about three years to reach $1M ARR ([Indie Hackers](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/we-just-reached-the-1m-arr-milestone-32722fc93e)). The published open-core conversion rates contradict each other and could not be traced to their original reports; plan on low single digits.

## Defence fits the product; medtech fits the price gap

Self-hosting demand in defence is regulation-driven. Jama describes air-gapped deployment as suited to aerospace and defence sectors that require offline solutions ([Jama](https://www.jamasoftware.com/deployment-options/)), and a cloud provider handling controlled unclassified information under CMMC must meet FedRAMP Moderate or equivalent ([Deltek](https://www.deltek.com/resources/articles/fedramp-impacts-cmmc/)). That is a hard barrier for small SaaS vendors and a structural reason for defence suppliers to self-host. Medtech has no equivalent rule, and its small buyers have a compliance reason to prefer a vendor-hosted tool. Atlassian adds a tailwind for any self-hosted positioning: Data Center stopped new-licence sales on 30 March 2026 and goes read-only on 28 March 2029, per consistent partner reports ([Seibert Group](https://us.seibert.group/blog/end-of-life-atlassian-data-center)).

The defence route has its own costs, and they are barely researched. Every incumbent already sells on-premises there, so self-hosting is expected, not novel. Sequoia-backed Flow Engineering and Stell target that sector ([Flow](https://flowengineering.com/blog/flow-raises-23m-from-sequoia-to-accelerate-the-future-of-hardware-development)). Buyers ask for offline licensing and vendor security documentation, AGPL will face legal review at primes, and no market sizing for small defence suppliers was found. The defence evidence comes largely from one vendor blog read as a search summary, and the CMMC dates conflict between sources.

The position this evidence supports: **a self-hosted-first product fits small defence and aerospace suppliers better than small medtech, and a medtech-first product should lead with hosted cloud, public prices and guaranteed export, with self-hosting as an option on every tier.** The founder holds a self-hosted product and a medtech beachhead, and those two choices pull against each other. Neither segment is validated, so the cheap move is to split the interviews: half with medtech teams of mixed hardware, software and quality staff (pure software teams have Jira, Ketryx and docs-as-code), half with small defence subcontractors facing CMMC Level 2. Nothing built so far is medtech-specific, so the choice can wait for that evidence. Automotive and European public-sector buyers were not researched at all.

## Conclusion

The research changes the pitch more than the product. "Open source, self-hosted, free reviewers, cheaper than Jama" is rebutted in a first sales call by Tuleap, Matrix Enterprise and Jama's own licensing. "RM-only with a Jama-style interface, public prices, no seat minimum, self-hostable on every tier, and your data always exportable" survives every finding here. The product's hard rules (append-only history, an audit event on every write) already supply the expensive half of a design-control record, which is why signed reviews and linked risk and test records are cheap to add and worth pulling forward.

The honest size of the prize is a small company: Tuleap and Matrix suggest low single-digit millions after a decade with a team, and nothing found shows a solo founder doing it. That is a fine outcome if it is the intended one. What the desk research cannot supply is the one fact that decides everything, whether a named buyer will pay a named price, and every source that could have hinted at it (Reddit, practitioner forums, gated industry reports, company registers, the FDA guidance text itself) went unread. Ten interviews and two paying design partners would outweigh this entire report.
